## Executive Summary & Strategic Integration Framework

This master deliverable represents the publication-grade academic, policy, and programmatic synthesis of the **Farmers' Action for Sustainable Agro-based Livelihoods (FASAL)** program. Operating in the remote, ecologically precarious, and administratively underserved buffer zone of the **Katarniaghat Wildlife Sanctuary** along the international border between India and Nepal, the FASAL project was conceived and implemented by the **Developmental Association for Human Advancement (DEHAT)** with primary grant support from **Azim Premji Philanthropic Initiatives (APPI)** under Grant Reference **G-2110-10970**.

The initiative was deployed across **6 Gram Panchayats (GPs)**—comprising **20 forest-fringe revenue and forest villages** characterized by indigenous settlements of the **Tharu Scheduled Tribe (ST)** alongside marginalized **Scheduled Caste (SC)**, **Other Backward Class (OBC)**, and vulnerable **single women**-headed farming families.

Over its operational lifecycle, FASAL unified grassroots rights-based community unionization, ecological farming transition under the **Low External Input Sustainable Agriculture (LEISA)** framework, high-value horticultural market linkages (notably the commercial turmeric value chain), decentralized citizen service delivery centers (**Jan Suvidha Kendras / CSCs**), and multi-stakeholder advocacy for public scheme convergence.

## Part 1: Executive & Programmatic Foundation

### 1.1 Grant Profile, Institutional History, and Socio-Ecological Context
The **Farmers' Action for Sustainable Agro-based Livelihoods (FASAL)** program represents an integrated, rights-based, agro-ecological livelihood intervention executed by the **Developmental Association for Human Advancement (DEHAT)** in eastern Uttar Pradesh, India. The initiative is funded by **Azim Premji Philanthropic Initiatives (APPI)** under Grant Agreement **G-2110-10970**, with an approved 5-year budget commitment of **₹2,16,00,000 (₹2.16 Crore)** covering the operational window of November 1, 2021 (active field rollout commencing January 1, 2022) through December 31, 2026.

DEHAT was established in 2000–2001 and registered under the Societies Registration Act XXI of 1860 (Registration No. 370/2000, dated 21 August 2000), with its headquarters located in Bahraich District, Uttar Pradesh. The organization’s roots trace back to approximately 1990 as an informal educational initiative serving indigenous Tharu children along the Indo-Nepal border, founded by the late social activist **Dr. Jitendra Chaturvedi**. Prior to the inception of FASAL, DEHAT had established three decades of deep-rooted community organizing along the border belt, building core competencies in child protection, anti-human trafficking, education for out-of-school girls, rescue and repatriation of bonded child laborers, and grassroots forest rights mobilization.

In conceptualizing FASAL, DEHAT recognized that extreme social vulnerability, child labor, trafficking susceptibility, and patriarchal violence in the border belt were fundamentally rooted in deep agrarian distress: unviable subsistence farming, lack of irrigation infrastructure, feudal extortion, and the near-total absence of democratic community institutions. FASAL was designed not as an isolated agronomic extension project, but as an integrated political-ecological program that unifies grassroots democratic collectivization, Low External Input Sustainable Agriculture (LEISA), public entitlement realization, and women-led community leadership.

Financial disbursement across the grant cycle followed a phased performance structure:
- **Year 1 (2022)**: ₹34,89,000 disbursed.
- **Year 2 (2023)**: ₹44,70,000 disbursed.
- **Year 3 (2024)**: ₹50,31,000 disbursed.
- **Year 4 (2025)**: ₹49,04,000 disbursed.
- **Cumulative Financial Balance (HY1–HY8)**: An unspent balance of ₹6,94,791 was maintained at the close of 2025, reflecting operational adjustments across programmatic lines (including recruitment delays in specialized technical positions and election-period code-of-conduct pauses). Of this balance, ₹2,88,643 in grant savings was formally proposed in late 2025 to be reallocated toward deploying a dedicated field data collection and survey enumeration team to digitize the physical Family Development Booklets.

### 1.2 Five Core Objectives and Strategic Theory of Change
As articulated in the project design (`Grant Application APPI_DEHAT V2.0 31st Aug.docx`) and operationalized in the 5-Year Results Framework (`RF Template (5year) Ver.2.0 16th Aug.xlsx` / `.md`), the FASAL program is structured around five core objectives:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                             FASAL THEORY OF CHANGE                               │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                                  │
│   [COMMUNITY INSTITUTION BUILDING]                                               │
│   74–80 Hamlet AAS ──► 5 Cluster Federations ──► 1 Sangharsh Block Federation   │
│   Doctrine: "Jiska Mudda Uski Agwai" (Direct Affected-Person Negotiation)        │
│                                │                                                 │
│                ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐                                 │
│                ▼                               ▼                                 │

│   - Soil Biology: Jeevamrit       - Jan Suvidha Kendra (JSK) Intermediation      │
│   - Non-Pesticide Management      - PRA-driven GPDP Micro-Planning               │
│   - Spatial Multi-Tier (Machan)   - Individual Rights (PMAY, Toilets, Pensions)  │

│   - Dietary Diversity & Resistance to Feudal-Bureaucratic Extortion               │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The specific parameters governing each core objective include:

1. **Outcome 1: Community Organization and Institution Building**:
   - Establish and sustain a three-tiered democratic federation: 74 to 80 hamlet-level *Aajeevika Adhikar Sangathans* (AAS / Livelihood Rights Collectives), 5 Cluster Federations (each coordinating ~15–16 AAS groups across 4 villages), and 1 apex *Sangharsh Block Level Federation* (BLF).
   - Mobilize 1,500 smallholder farming households into active membership, maintaining greater than 50% female participation in regular monthly governance meetings.
   - Institutionalize Participatory Rural Appraisal (PRA) micro-planning to draft bottom-up Gram Panchayat Development Plans (GPDPs), presenting them directly to Gram Sabhas and block/district administrations.

2. **Outcome 2: Agricultural Intensification, Food Security, & Income Enhancement**:
   - Improve household grain self-sufficiency from a baseline level of approximately 5 quintals per Bigha per year toward a target of 7.5 to 8 quintals of grain per Bigha per year (supplemented by 3 to 4 quintals of seasonal Zaid vegetables), moving families closer to meeting their annual consumption requirement of ~12 quintals per family of five.
   - Reduce the summer fallow gap (where approximately 75% of cultivable land lay fallow during Zaid) by introducing heat-tolerant cucurbits, pulses, and green vegetables.
   - Elevate the net agricultural income of participating smallholders toward the APPI benchmark of ₹60,000 per acre/annum through Crop Intensification Systems (CIS), line sowing, multi-tier vertical farming (*Machan* systems), and Low External Input Sustainable Agriculture (LEISA) bio-inputs.

3. **Outcome 3: Human Capital Development via Community Resource Persons**:
   - Identify, train, and deploy a decentralized grassroots cadre of 80 Community Resource Persons (CRPs / *Krishi Mitras*), identified directly from all 80 AAS groups across the 20 villages (~4 per village).
   - Implement an extension model where Krishi Mitras provide localized agronomic advisory, demonstration management, and input preparation mentoring to neighboring farming families, supported by specialized training of lead farmer cohorts (5 farmers per village).

4. **Outcome 4: Citizen-Led Entitlement Realization & Public Leverage**:
   - Establish and operate a community-accessible Jan Suvidha Kendra (JSK / Citizen Service Centre) at the block level and institutionalize regular administrative interface meetings at village, block, and district tiers.
   - Facilitate citizen access to individual welfare entitlements (MGNREGA job cards, PM-KISAN, pensions, PMAY housing, and Swachh Bharat toilets) alongside public infrastructure investments (concrete village roads, Jal Jeevan Mission piped water, and drainage works) through systematic convergence with government departments.

5. **Outcome 5: Organizational Capacity Building & Systems Development**:
   - Institutionalize program management, financial accounting, and field monitoring systems within DEHAT.
   - Foster institutional learning and technical exchange with premier civil society and technical partners, including Samaj Pragati Sahyog (SPS, Madhya Pradesh), Rythu Sadhikara Samstha (RySS, Andhra Pradesh), and VAAGDHARA (Rajasthan), while maintaining proposed technical linkages with the Acharya Narendra Deva University of Agriculture & Technology (ANDUAT Crop Research Station, Bahraich) and Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK Bahraich).

### 1.4 Demographic Vulnerability, Marginality, and Target Populations
The socio-demographic baseline of the FASAL target community, as established through external empirical diagnostics covering 1,504 farmers (`Baseline Report - Jaykar.pdf`), portrays a population characterized by severe structural exclusion:

- **Social Category & Caste Disaggregation**:
  - **Other Backward Classes (OBC)**: Comprise **61.0%** of the farming population, representing smallholders and tenant sharecroppers heavily burdened by debt-financed chemical agricultural inputs.
  - **Scheduled Tribes (ST)**: Comprise **26.0%** of the population, constituted almost entirely by indigenous **Tharu tribals** residing along the forest periphery. The Tharu community possesses rich traditional ecological knowledge but has historically faced bureaucratic marginalization, delayed land tenure formalization under the Forest Rights Act (FRA 2006), and routine harassment by lower forestry personnel.
  - **Scheduled Castes (SC)**: Comprise **13.0%** of the population, representing asset-poor households concentrated in agricultural wage labour, informal brick-kiln employment, and insecure oral sharecropping.
  - **Religious Breakdown**: 98.0% of households identify as Hindu and 2.0% as Muslim, reflecting an agrarian demographic embedded in traditional rural social relations.

- **Gender, Marital Status & Single Women Vulnerability**:
  - 89.0% of respondents were married, living within patriarchal joint or nuclear households where women historically exercised limited formal authority over land titles, crop sales, or capital allocations.
  - **Widows and Single Women**: Exactly **10.0% of all surveyed respondents were widows**, with another **1.0% unmarried**. Single women in Mihinpurwa face severe structural barriers: patriarchal inheritance exclusion, customary pressure from marital kin, total absence of land collateral to access institutional credit, and heightened economic insecurity. FASAL established an explicit priority mandate targeting single women and female-headed households for homestead nutrition gardens, poultry units, and social welfare pension enrollment.

- **Demographic Structure & Household Dependency**:
  - Total enumerated household members across the 1,504 baseline families stood at **6,852 individuals** (3,488 males, 3,278 females; an average household size of 5.0 members; note the baseline text's internal sum of 6,766).
  - The dominant age demographic of primary respondents was concentrated between **27 and 36 years (434 individuals / 28.9%)** and **37 to 46 years (386 individuals / 25.7%)**, representing a productive but economically stressed working-age cohort facing heavy dependency burdens from young children and elderly family members.

- **Baseline Agrarian and Economic Vulnerability**:
  - **Micro-Holding Domination**: **71.08% of farmers cultivated micro-holdings between 0.1 and 5.1 Bighas** (0.02 to 1.02 acres, based on the local standard of 5 Bighas = 1 Acre). Smallholders cultivating 5.1 to 10.1 Bighas (1.02 to 2.02 acres) represented 20.68%. Only 7.38% held medium landholdings (10.1 to 30.1 Bighas), while larger landholders (>30.1 Bighas / >6.0 acres) represented a minuscule 0.73% (11 farmers).
  - **Tenancy and Landlessness**: 8.0% of farming households were completely landless, relying entirely on expensive, informal oral lease tenancy (sharecropping / *Batai*), while 22.0% of landholding farmers leased in supplementary land to achieve basic household subsistence.
  - **Irrigation Asymmetry and Diesel Dependence**: 65.29% (982 farmers) depended entirely on hired, diesel-powered mobile pump engines, paying high hourly rental rates (averaging ₹150/hour) that consumed a major share of their gross crop expenditure. Another 33.98% (511 farmers) had no irrigation access and were completely dependent on erratic monsoon rainfall. Only 10 farmers (0.66%) possessed permanent pumping sets, and **exactly one single farmer (0.07%) owned a solar-powered irrigation pump** across the entire 20-village footprint.
  - **Sub-Subsistence Staple Economics**: Baseline net returns from cereal monocultures were low: Kharif paddy generated an average net profit of merely **₹2,720.30 per acre** (against an average production cost of ₹10,449.36 and yield of 10.97 quintals/acre), while Rabi wheat yielded a net profit of only **₹3,657.23 per acre** (cost of ₹22,419.35, yield of 48.61 quintals/acre).
  - **High Chemical Dependency**: Over 70.6% of farmers applied synthetic urea to paddy and 75.8% applied urea to wheat; 67.8% applied DAP to paddy and 73.5% to wheat. In stark contrast, organic farmyard manure (FYM) composting was practiced by only 10.4%, green manuring was utilized by fewer than 2.8%, and **bio-fertilizers were used by less than 1.9% of farmers**.
  - **Entitlement Exclusion and Institutional Inertia**: While 97.0% possessed a bank account (primarily opened under government saturation drives), and 64.6% held BPL/MGNREGA cards, foundational welfare schemes were bottlenecked: PM-KISAN reached only 10.9% (164 farmers), old-age pensions reached only 1.1% (17 individuals), and widow pensions reached only 0.6% (9 widows). Crucially, while 1,274 individuals had historical associations with Self-Help Groups (SHGs), **100% of respondents reported that they never attended monthly meetings**, illustrating that prior village institutions were largely dormant.

## Part 2: Institutional Collectivization & Democratic Federation Architecture

### 2.1 The Three-Tier Federation Model (AAS, Cluster, Sangharsh BLF)
The central operational innovation of the FASAL program is its refusal to treat smallholder farmers as passive recipients of external agricultural technology. Instead, DEHAT recognized that without democratic institutional power, marginal farmers would remain structurally incapable of defending their land tenure, negotiating fair market prices, accessing public scheme entitlements, or resisting bureaucratic extortion.

The institutional structure was deliberately architected as a nested, democratic, three-tier federation:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                     THREE-TIER FEDERATION ARCHITECTURE                          │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                                 │
│   TIER 3: APEX BLOCK LEVEL FEDERATION (BLF)                                     │
│   [ SANGHARSH BLOCK LEVEL FEDERATION ]                                          │
│   - Represents 1,500 farming families across all 6 Gram Panchayats              │
│   - Governed by Executive Committee elected from 5 Cluster Federations          │
│   - Mandate: Block/District Administrative Interfaces (Tehsil Diwas), Policy    │
│     Advocacy, Public Accountability, FPO Oversight                              │
│                                      ▲                                          │
│                                      │                                          │
│   TIER 2: CLUSTER LEVEL FEDERATIONS (5 CLUSTERS)                                │
│   [ Ekta | Gramin Vikas | Hariom | Divyajyoti | Shanti/Gramin Vikas ]           │
│   - Coordinates ~4 villages / ~15–16 AAS groups per cluster                     │
│   - Physical Cluster Offices established as community mobilization nodes        │
│   - Mandate: Inter-village conflict resolution, collective input aggregation,   │
│     turmeric/seed aggregation, review of Krishi Mitras                          │
│                                      ▲                                          │
│                                      │                                          │
│   TIER 1: PRIMARY HAMLET COLLECTIVES (74 – 80 AAS)                              │
│   [ AAJEEVIKA ADHIKAR SANGATHANS - AAS ]                                        │
│   - Hyper-local village/hamlet units comprising 15–20 small & marginal farmers  │
│   - Regular monthly meetings on fixed calendar dates with written registers     │
│   - >50% active female attendance; peer leadership by fellow farmers            │
│   - Mandate: Micro-planning, Jeevamrit preparation, local grievance registers,  │
│     PDS quota monitoring, mutual support                                        │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

#### Tier 1: Aajeevika Adhikar Sangathans (AAS)
The foundational building blocks are the hamlet-level *Aajeevika Adhikar Sangathans* (AAS / Livelihood Rights Organizations). Each AAS convenes 15 to 20 contiguous farming families within a specific hamlet (e.g., Roshani AAS, Maa AAS in Bhattha; Shanti AAS, Sheela AAS in Jamuniha; Meera AAS in Vishun Tanda; Ganga AAS in Hazaripurwa; Ambe AAS in Kailashnagar; Sadhana Azadi AAS, Sheetal AAS in Rampurwa; Ravidas AAS, Soorsati AAS in Mangalpurwa; Jwala AAS, Bala AAS in Badhiyanpurwa).

The operational discipline of an AAS includes:
- Monthly meetings held at a fixed time, location, and date, reducing reliance on external NGO organizers to call meetings.
- Democratic governance managed by elected female office bearers: a President (*Adhyaksh*), Secretary (*Sachiv*), and Treasurer (*Koshadhyaksh*).
- Maintenance of standardized physical registers tracking: (a) member attendance, (b) agronomic seasonal commitments (area dedicated to LEISA bio-inputs, machan trellises, or turmeric), and (c) citizen rights grievance logs (ration card discrepancies, non-functioning handpumps, missing pension payments).

#### Tier 2: The Five Cluster Federations
To bridge the structural gap between isolated village hamlets and the block-level administration, DEHAT organized the AAS groups into five geographically clustered federations:
1. **Ekta Cluster Federation** (Centering Bajpur Bankati and Barkharia GPs).
2. **Gramin Vikas Cluster Federation** (Centering Karikot GP - Jamuniha and Bhattha nodes).
3. **Hariom Cluster Federation** (Centering Chahalwa GP - Bhorahanpurwa, Mangalpurwa, Sirsiyanpurwa nodes).
4. **Divyajyoti Cluster Federation** (Centering Fakirpuri GP - Fakirpuri and Rampurwa nodes).
5. **Shanti / Gramin Vikas Cluster Federation** (Centering Rajaram Tanda and adjoining settlements; note that Sharda is an AAS group in Jamuniha, not a separate cluster).

Each Cluster Federation maintains a village office (inaugurated with community participation, documented in photographic archives `Cluster office inauguration.jpg`), governed by a council composed of elected delegates (up to 3 leaders per AAS group per the Results Framework). The clusters meet monthly to coordinate collective seed banking, review Krishi Mitra extension performance, aggregate farm produce (such as organic turmeric for FPO processing), and resolve inter-hamlet disputes.

#### Tier 3: Sangharsh Block Level Federation (BLF)
The apex institution of the FASAL architecture is the **Sangharsh Block Level Federation**. Composed of executive representatives elected from all five clusters, the Sangharsh BLF represents the collective civic voice of 1,500 forest-fringe farming families across Mihinpurwa Block.

The Sangharsh BLF convenes regularly at the block level to:
- Directly interface with block and district officials (Block Development Officer [BDO], Sub-Divisional Magistrate [SDM], Assistant Development Officer [ADO-Panchayat], Junior Engineers, and Forest Rangers) during formal interface meetings and Tehsil Diwas assemblies.
- Review public service delivery bottlenecks across the 6 Gram Panchayats.
- Author, endorse, and track legal representations and public grievance complaints regarding administrative irregularities, forest department friction, and MGNREGA wage delays.
- Connect farmers with the community-based Farmer Producer Organization (*DEHAT Sondhi Maati Farmer Producer Company Limited*).

### 2.2 The Operational Doctrine of "Jiska Mudda Uski Agwai"
The foundational pedagogical and governance principle underpinning the FASAL collective model is **"Jiska Mudda Uski Agwai"** — literally translated as *"Whose issue it is, theirs is the leadership."*

In conventional development programs, external NGO staff or local dominant intermediaries frequently act as self-appointed representatives, speaking on behalf of marginalized communities. DEHAT deliberately departed from this paradigm. The doctrine of *Jiska Mudda Uski Agwai* establishes that:
1. **Direct Negotiation**: The individuals who directly experience structural exclusion—a tribal woman denied her monthly food rations, a forest dweller facing harassment from forest guards, a widow denied her pension—must formulate the demand, sit at the negotiation table, and address public authorities in their own voice.
2. **Pedagogical Demystification of Power**: DEHAT facilitators act as institutional coaches rather than spokespersons. Facilitators provide legal literacy, explain administrative procedures, rehearse dialogue through role-play and applied theatre (drawing on Forum Theatre / Theatre of the Oppressed pedagogy), and accompany community members to government offices. During formal meetings with administrative officials, community women lead the dialogue directly.
3. **Mitigating Elite Capture**: In rural Bahraich, local power dynamics are heavily influenced by the *Pradhan-Pati* phenomenon, large landholders, and local intermediaries. By mandating that the specific individual affected by a grievance leads the representation, the federation neutralizes intermediaries, ensuring that administrative resolutions build lasting personal dignity and civic agency for marginalized women.

### 2.3 Participatory Micro-Planning: PRA, Gram Sabhas, and GPDP Formulation
Under the 73rd Constitutional Amendment, the Gram Sabha possesses statutory authority to formulate the annual **Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP)**, determining the allocation of public funds across rural infrastructure, social welfare, and natural resource management. However, in practice across the region, GPDPs were frequently drafted without broad citizen participation, bypassing marginalized forest hamlets.

FASAL democratized the GPDP planning process through a structured participatory methodology:

```

├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                                                 │
│   STAGE 1: PARTICIPATORY RURAL APPRAISAL (PRA) AT HAMLET TIER                   │
│   - AAS groups execute Social Mapping, Resource Mapping, and Seasonal Calendars │
│   - Identification of infrastructure deficits: muddy access roads,             │
│     waterlogged lowlands, missing handpumps, school repair needs                │
│                                      │                                          │
│                                      ▼                                          │
│   STAGE 2: HAMLET CHARTER CONSOLIDATION & CITIZEN RESOLUTIONS                   │
│   - Demands synthesized into written Hamlet Development Charters                │
│   - Resolutions endorsed by signatures and thumbprints of AAS women             │
│   - Rehearsal of dialogue using Applied Theatre methods                         │
│                                      │                                          │
│                                      ▼                                          │
│   STAGE 3: COLLECTIVE GRAM SABHA PRESENTATION                                   │
│   - Citizen mobilization: 1,712 citizens participated across 5 GPs              │
│   - Women leaders formally present signed charters to Pradhan and Secretary     │
│   - Advocacy for inclusion of hamlet proposals into official GPDP drafts        │
│                                      │                                          │

```

The application of this methodology was demonstrated in Year 3 (2024), when **1,712 citizens** participated across 5 Gram Panchayats (Fakirpuri: 274, Vishunapur: 381, Karikot: 522, Chahalwa: 302, and Bajpur Bankati: 233, as documented in Grant Report 2024, Footnote 1). Citizens mapped local infrastructure priorities and formally submitted community charters to Gram Pradhans and Panchayat Secretaries for incorporation into official GPDP proposals.

### 2.4 Intergenerational Social Architecture: Bal Sansad, Kishor Sangathan, and Jitendra Youth Fellowship
Recognizing that entrenched social norms, child marriage, and human trafficking vulnerabilities cannot be dismantled solely through adult farmer assemblies, DEHAT embedded a complementary intergenerational social architecture:

1. **Bal Sansad (Children's Parliaments)**:
   - Established across intervention villages (tracking 9 active village parliaments in monthly review decks), the Bal Sansad organizes children aged 6 to 14.
   - Operating under democratic roles (including Prime Minister, Education Minister, and Health/Water Minister), children participate in peer tracking of primary school attendance, monitor midday meal quality, and discuss child safety in village environments.
2. **Kishor & Kishori Sangathans (Adolescent Collectives)**:
   - Mobilizing adolescent girls and boys aged 15 to 19, these collectives provide structured discussions on gender equality, menstrual hygiene awareness, legal marriageable age, and digital literacy.
   - The Kishori collectives serve as village-level awareness networks against child marriage and human trafficking, interfacing with local police stations and Sashastra Seema Bal (SSB) border personnel on trafficking prevention.
3. **Jitendra Youth Fellowship (JYF)**:
   - A dedicated 2-year leadership fellowship for rural youth, established in memory of DEHAT's late founder, Dr. Jitendra Chaturvedi.
   - Drawing upon Forum Theatre and "Theatre of the Oppressed" pedagogies, the fellowship equips rural youth to examine local socio-economic issues, conduct street theatre on wage rights, domestic violence, and bonded labour, and mobilize peers into active community participation.
   - *Institutional Delineation*: While the JYF, Bal Sansad, and adolescent collectives engage the same community geography, they operate under DEHAT's broader Human Protection and Rights and Entitlement portfolio (supported through independent funding streams from Dasra and She's the First), functioning as synergistic parallel initiatives alongside FASAL's agricultural core.

## Part 3: Chronological Implementation Trajectory (2021–2026)

### 3.1 Year 1 (2021–2022): Inception, External Baseline Diagnostics, and Systemic Shocks
- **Grant Documentation**: `Grant Application APPI_DEHAT V2.0 31st Aug.docx`, `Baseline Report - Jaykar.pdf`, `FASAL 2022\Narrative Report HY2.pdf`.
- **Operational Reality**: Year 1 focused on field mobilization and baseline diagnostics across the 20 intervention villages. DEHAT conducted an extensive baseline enumeration covering 1,504 farmers to establish benchmark socio-economic indicators across the 6 Gram Panchayats.
- **Ecological and Operational Challenges**:
  - *Monsoon Flooding*: Heavy monsoon rains and river swelling from the Saryu and Sharda river basins disrupted road transit across Mihinpurwa Block, temporarily isolating low-lying hamlets (including Dhodhepurwa, Sirsiyanpurwa, and Jamuniha). Floodwaters caused closures of local mobile network towers and damaged rural transit routes, complicating field communication.
  - *Human-Wildlife Encounters*: The proximity of project villages to the buffer zone of the Katarniaghat Wildlife Sanctuary brought recurring human-wildlife encounters. Wild elephants and wild herbivores (such as deer and wild boars) caused significant crop damage in forest-edge fields. The physical danger and remote conditions led to a higher turnover rate among newly recruited female field team members appointed from nearby urban districts. DEHAT responded by establishing stronger staff support systems, adapting travel protocols, and actively recruiting frontline facilitators from local communities.
  - *Forest Department Friction*: In Kailashnagar (historically known as *Dhekia Vangram*, formally converted to revenue village status by the state government in January 2022), local forest personnel resisted the construction of permanent brick houses and sanitary toilets under PMAY, asserting jurisdictional claims and delaying early village infrastructure work.
- **Ground-Level Community Actions**:
  - *Addressing PDS Grain Discrepancies in Fakirpuri*: In response to a local fair-price shop dealer (*Kotedar*) distributing only 3 kg of ration instead of the statutory 5 kg per unit, mobilized women of the Fakirpuri AAS organized a collective representation. Documenting the shortfall, community women took the issue to the district administration, resulting in an administrative inquiry, the dismissal of the corrupt dealer, and formal apologies from contractors in Bardia and Hazaripurwa.
  - *Community Consensus on Infrastructure in Dhodhepurwa*: In Dhodhepurwa, where rainy season waterlogging routinely rendered the village track impassable, the Gram Panchayat allocated ₹1,19,000 for an RCC road. To resolve disputes over road width and drainage alignment, the local AAS facilitated community dialogues, leading participating families to voluntarily adjust their homestead boundaries to allow construction of a functional elevated road and drainage system.

### 3.2 Year 2 (2023): Institutional Consolidation, Anti-Extortion Battles (*Begari* & *Tihai*), and Legal Mobilization
- **Grant Documentation**: `FASAL_2023\HY4 Fluxx Part-I Report.docx`, `FASAL_2023\HY4 Report-Remaining Aspects.pdf`, `HY4 Report-Remaining Aspects.pdf`.
- **Operational Reality**: Year 2 consolidated the three-tier federation structure and saw community collectives take organized action against informal feudal levies enforced in the forest-border belt. Cumulative public scheme and infrastructure leverage mobilized surpassed **₹3.04 Crore**, while women's participation in monthly AAS assemblies stabilized above 50%.
- **Challenging Informal Forestry Levies**:
  - For generations, forest-border communities lived under informal, unwritten levies enforced by lower forestry personnel:
    1. ***Begari* (Unpaid Labour)**: Forest dwellers were routinely subjected to demands for uncompensated labour, including clearing forest fire-lines, assisting in timber transport, and maintaining forest outposts under threat of movement restrictions.
    2. ***Tihai* (Crop Levy)**: Lower forest personnel enforced an informal levy demanding **10 kg of grain per Bigha** at harvest time from fringe farmers in exchange for unhindered access to dry fuelwood collection and protection against arbitrary First Information Reports (FIRs) alleging illegal forest entry.
  - *The Collective Response*: AAS women and federation leaders methodically documented instances of extortion, recording dates, demanded grain quantities, and personnel details.
  - *Administrative Representations and Transfers*: Armed with primary evidence, community delegations escalated formal complaints to senior district authorities. Subsequent administrative scrutiny into regional irregularities resulted in the **transfer of both the Divisional Forest Officer (DFO) and the Chief Development Officer (CDO) of Bahraich District**, with further investigations initiated by the incoming administration.
  - *Retaliatory Pressure*: Following these actions, local rangers registered retaliatory timber-theft cases against select community leaders, unpruned hazardous trees overhanging the DEHAT office were left unattended, and officials occasionally entered community meeting spaces to question participants.
  - *Legal Mobilization*: Drawing inspiration from the legal struggle of tribal women in **Vachathi, Tamil Nadu** (who pursued landmark judicial accountability against official atrocities), the Sangharsh BLF prepared legal complaints under the **Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989** and asserted customary forest rights provisions under the **Forest Rights Act, 2006 (FRA)**.
- **Resisting Commercial Predation**:
  - Commercial sales representatives sought to utilize the expanding AAS network as a distribution channel, attempting to recruit Krishi Mitras and AAS members on commission to sell commercial packaged bio-fertilizers, private sanitary products, and tonics. AAS leadership recognized the conflict of interest, holding discussions that reinforced organizational independence and community-based input production.
- **Permanent Construction in Kailashnagar**:
  - Overcoming administrative resistance, the formal recognition of Kailashnagar enabled the historic construction of the village’s first permanent brick PMAY houses, individual toilets, and paved brick tracks.

### 3.3 Year 3 (2024): Scaled GPDP Micro-Planning, LEISA Dissemination, and Market Exploration
- **Grant Documentation**: `Grant Report January to December 2024.pdf`, `FASAL_2024\Grant Report template_Draft 1.docx`, 11 Monthly PPTX Presentations (`FASAL_2024\Presentations FASAL_2024\`).
- **Operational Reality**: Year 3 emphasized participatory local governance planning and the broader adoption of Low External Input Sustainable Agriculture (LEISA) practices.
- **Participatory Planning Across Gram Panchayats**:
  - Engaging **1,712 citizens** across 5 Gram Panchayats through PRA exercises, community members mapped village priorities, identifying drainage bottlenecks, drinking water needs, and agrarian infrastructure requirements for formal submission to Gram Sabhas.
  - Annual government scheme leverage recorded in the 2024 register totaled **₹2,29,93,200 (₹2.30 Crore)** across 28 convergence categories, including brick roads, drainage soak pits, and school infrastructural support.
- **LEISA Agronomic Dissemination**:
  - Technical training in sustainable agriculture engaged **75 Krishi Mitras** in specialized pest management and LEISA modules, generating **603 cumulative farmer participations** across LEISA, mixed cropping, multilayer farming, and turmeric workshops (Grant Report 2024, Footnote 2). Farmers adopted biological preparations, including *Jeevamrit*, dry *Ghanjeevamrit*, and botanical sprays (*Char Chatni* and *Neemastra*).
  - Introduction of bamboo-and-wire vertical trellis cultivation (*Machan*) for climbing cucurbits intercropped with shade-tolerant spices (turmeric and ginger).
- **Market Linkage & Farmer Recognition**:
  - 40 smallholders participated in the District Kisan Mela in Bahraich, where 2 FASAL women farmers were recognized with Certificates of Appreciation (*Prashasti Patra*) presented by the District Magistrate and local Member of Legislative Assembly (MLA).
  - Value-chain linkage discussions were initiated with institutional buyers, including Patanjali Ayurved and the Uttar Pradesh Department of Horticulture, for organic turmeric aggregation through the *Sondhi Maati Farmer Producer Company*.

### 3.4 Year 4 (2025): Midline Transformation Panel (N=1,328), Commercial Value Chains, and Organizational Resilience
- **Grant Documentation**: `Midline_FASAL_Jaykar (1).docx`, `Grant Report January to December 2025.md` / `.pdf`, 17 Monthly PPTX Presentations (`FASAL_2025\Presentations FASAL_2025\`).
- **Operational Reality**: Year 4 provided empirical documentation of household income and dietary shifts, while simultaneously testing DEHAT’s organizational resilience during institutional funding transitions.
- **Empirical Midline Evaluation Findings (Recall Sample N=1,328)**:
  - *Upward Income Trends*: The external evaluation survey analyzing multi-year recall across 1,328 households recorded that the proportion of farmers in the lowest income bracket (earning **<₹50,000 per annum**) declined from **81.2% (1,079 households in 2022) to 61.1% (812 households in 2024)**. Conversely, households earning **₹50,000 to ₹2,00,000 annually expanded from 18.6% (247 households) to 36.8% (487 households)**. ## Part 4: Sustainable Agronomic Package of Practices (PoP) & Ecological Intensification

### 4.1 Theoretical Paradigm: Transiting from HIESA to LEISA
For three decades, agricultural development across eastern Uttar Pradesh was dominated by the high-input Green Revolution paradigm, often characterized as **High External Input Sustainable Agriculture (HIESA)**. This system centered on commercial hybrid seeds, petroleum-dependent diesel irrigation, synthetic nitrogenous fertilizers (Urea), diammonium phosphate (DAP), and synthetic chemical pesticides.

In the forest-fringe ecology of Mihinpurwa Block, this input-intensive model generated a severe agrarian vulnerability trap:
1. *Soil Health Depletion*: Continual reliance on synthetic chemical fertilizers depleted natural soil organic matter and beneficial soil organisms, leading to soil compaction and diminished water retention capacity.
2. *The Chemical Debt Cycle*: Input costs escalated while staple cereal prices remained volatile. Baseline findings indicated that smallholders spent an average of ₹10,449 per acre to cultivate Kharif paddy, yielding an average net profit of merely ₹2,720 per acre, while spending ₹22,419 per acre on Rabi wheat to achieve an average net profit of only ₹3,657 per acre. Any unexpected climatic shock—such as monsoon flooding or unseasonal hailstorms—compounded household indebtedness.

FASAL sought to address this dependency through **Low External Input Sustainable Agriculture (LEISA)**:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    THE AGRONOMIC PARADIGM SHIFT (HIESA TO LEISA)                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│   HIGH EXTERNAL INPUT (HIESA)               FASAL / LEISA FRAMEWORK             │
│   - Synthetic Urea & DAP                     - Bio-Nutrients (Jeevamrit)         │
│   - Chemical Pesticides                      - Botanical Sprays (Char Chatni)    │
│   - Single-Layer Monoculture (Paddy/Wheat)   - Multi-Tier Trellis (Machan)       │
│   - Summer Fallow (~75% Land Unused)         - Zaid Cropping (Cucurbits/Pulses)  │
│   - Commercial Seed Dependency               - Local Seed Conservation & Banks   │
│   - Soil Biology Degradation                 - On-Farm Microbial Soil Care       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The LEISA approach emphasizes maximizing on-farm and community-derived biological resources. Cattle dung and urine, leguminous pulses, crop residues, and locally available botanical leaves replace expensive synthetic commercial inputs, reducing out-of-pocket agricultural expenditures while rebuilding natural soil vitality.

### 4.2 On-Farm Bio-Nutrient Formulations: Jeevamrit, Ghanjeevamrit, and Soil Biology Regeneration

#### 1. Liquid Jeevamrit (Microbial Bio-Inoculant)
*Jeevamrit* is utilized across project hamlets as an on-farm biological culture that supports soil microbial life and accelerates nutrient cycling in the rhizosphere.
- **Locally Sourced Ingredients**:
  - Fresh indigenous cow dung (*Desi Gobar*), supplying beneficial microflora.
  - Cow urine (*Gomutra*), providing organic nitrogen, mineral compounds, and natural enzymes.
  - Organic jaggery (*Gud*), serving as a readily available carbon energy source.
  - Pulse flour (*Besan*, typically from chickpea, pigeon pea, or cowpea), providing protein for microbial growth.
  - Native soil (*Medh ki Mitti*), acting as an undisturbed mother culture of local soil microorganisms.
  - Clean water.
- **Community Preparation & Infrastructure**:
  - Participating farmers prepare the formulation inside 200-liter plastic drums distributed under the project (documented in project presentations, including `12. NRM_DEHAT_जनवरी २०२५.pptx`, Slides 22 & 56, and December 2023 decks).
  - The ingredients are combined and stirred twice daily, covered with a breathable jute bag, and kept in shaded areas to ferment for 48 to 72 hours before field application.
- **Field Application**:
  - Applied as a soil drench along irrigation channels or strained for foliar spray across vegetables, pulses, and field crops.

#### 2. Dry Ghanjeevamrit (Basal Bio-Fertilizer)
For rainfed fields, upland plots, or pre-sowing soil conditioning where liquid applications are difficult to manage, farmers prepare solid *Ghanjeevamrit*:
- **Preparation & Use**:
  - Prepared by blending pulverized, shade-dried cow dung with liquid Jeevamrit (or cow urine, jaggery, and pulse flour).
  - The mixture is thoroughly turned, covered, and fermented in the shade before being air-dried and sieved.
  - Applied as a basal dressing during field plowing and bed preparation, supplementing organic farmyard manure.

#### 3. Improved Farmyard Manuring (FYM) & Covered Compost Pits
- Baseline surveys indicated that farmers frequently kept cattle dung in open, unshaded heaps where rain leached nutrients and sun exposure caused nitrogen loss. Through project workshops (engaging 603 cumulative farmer participations across 2024 training modules per Grant Report 2024, Footnote 2), farmers were trained to construct covered, shaded compost pits, layering farmyard biomass, cow dung, and crop residues to preserve organic humus.

#### 4. Green Manuring (*Dhaincha* & Summer Legumes)
- Prior to Kharif paddy transplantation, farmers are encouraged to incorporate green manuring crops such as *Dhaincha* (*Sesbania bispinosa*) or sunn hemp (*Sanai*). Sown with pre-monsoon showers and incorporated into the soil during field preparation prior to flowering, green manures contribute organic biomass and atmospheric nitrogen to the soil.

### 4.3 Non-Pesticide Management (NPM): Char Chatni, 10 Parani Ark, and Neemastra
To eliminate synthetic chemical sprays that pose health risks to farming families and eliminate beneficial predators in the field ecosystem, FASAL promoted locally prepared botanical Non-Pesticide Management (NPM) formulations:

### 4.4 Agronomic & Spatial Intensification: CIS Line Sowing, Trellis Multi-Tier (*Machan*) Cultivation, and Zaid Cropping

#### 1. Crop Intensification Systems (CIS Line Sowing)
Rather than traditional manual broadcasting, which leads to uneven plant densities and high seed mortality, FASAL promoted Crop Intensification Systems (CIS) across paddy and wheat:
- *Line Sowing & Reduced Seed Density*: In accordance with the project grant design (`Grant Application APPI_DEHAT V2.0 31st Aug.docx`), seeds are sown in defined straight lines with **10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm) spacing between plants and rows**, rather than broadcast haphazardly.
- *Aeration and Light Interception*: This geometric spacing allows adequate sunlight penetration to the base of the crop canopy, facilitates manual weeding, reduces microclimate humidity that fosters fungal pathogens, and supports robust root system expansion.

#### 2. Machan (Vertical Trellis) Multi-Tier Cultivation
In a region where approximately 71% of farmers cultivate micro-holdings smaller than 1 acre (5 Bighas), horizontal land expansion is structurally impossible. FASAL introduced vertical multi-tier farming using bamboo and wire/nylon trellises (*Machan*):

#### 3. Zaid (Summer) Cropping: Addressing the Seasonal Fallow
At project baseline, approximately 75% of cultivable land remained fallow during the pre-monsoon summer months (April to June) due to extreme heat and diesel irrigation expenses. FASAL introduced heat-tolerant, short-duration summer crops:
- Climbing and trailing cucurbits (including bitter gourd, cucumber, and bottle gourd) grown with organic mulching to conserve rhizosphere moisture.
- Summer leguminous pulses (such as cowpea / *Lobia* and moong), which provide fresh green pods for household nutrition, contribute nutritious pulse grain, supply animal fodder, and fix atmospheric nitrogen during the lean summer period.

### 4.6 Bund Agroforestry as a Design Objective
In eastern Uttar Pradesh, firewood scarcity frequently compels rural households to utilize cattle dung cakes (*Kande*) for cooking fuel, depriving fields of valuable organic manure. To address this structural challenge, the FASAL project design (`Grant Application APPI_DEHAT V2.0 31st Aug.docx`) proposed **Bund Agroforestry**:
- Encouraging the planting of fast-growing, multi-purpose leguminous trees and shrubs (such as *Gliricidia* and *Acacia*) along field borders (*Mendhs*).
- Field bund plantations serve as boundary windbreaks, help stabilize soil edges during heavy rains, and produce lopped branches for domestic cooking fuel, thereby enabling households to divert a greater share of cattle manure toward on-farm composting and bio-fertilizer preparation.

## Part 5: Operational Struggles, Political Economy, and Organizational Shocks

### 5.1 Human-Wildlife Conflicts and Frontline Staff Turnover
The operational environment of Mihinpurwa Block is profoundly shaped by its location along the immediate boundary of the Katarniaghat Wildlife Sanctuary. The sanctuary's diverse wildlife populations regularly interact with surrounding agricultural hamlets:
- **Wildlife Incursions and Crop Depredation**: Herds of wild elephants moving along traditional forest corridors cause severe damage to standing cereal and sugarcane crops, while wild boars and deer dig up root crops and forage in vegetable fields.
- **Frontline Staff Turnover**: The extreme physical isolation, difficult transit conditions along unpaved forest trails, and recurring wildlife encounters created significant stress for frontline project personnel. In Year 1, DEHAT experienced a higher turnover rate among female team members who had been recruited from nearby urban districts and found the remote forest-border conditions untenable.
- **Adaptive Organizational Measures**: DEHAT responded by strengthening field support structures, providing gender-sensitive security protocols, establishing flexible transit arrangements, and prioritizing the recruitment and leadership of local community women and Krishi Mitras who possessed deep familiarity with the local forest landscape.

### 5.2 The Kailashnagar Timber-Theft Crisis: Criminalization of Grassroots Collectives and Legal Resistance
In Kailashnagar (historically *Dhekia Vangram*), which was formally granted revenue village status by the Government of Uttar Pradesh in January 2022, the local forestry bureaucracy initially contested the administrative transition:
- **Administrative Friction**: Forest department personnel sought to restrict the movement of building materials and actively obstructed community members from constructing permanent brick-and-mortar homes and sanitary toilets under Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY).
- **Retaliatory Legal Harassment**: When community collectives organized under the AAS protested these obstructions, lower forestry personnel threatened community members with fabricated First Information Reports (FIRs) alleging illicit timber theft from the sanctuary.
- **Collective Resolution**: Through coordinated representations organized by the Sangharsh BLF at the tehsil and district levels, the community established their statutory rights as revenue village residents, clearing the way for the historic construction of the village's first permanent brick houses, sanitation facilities, and paved brick pathways.

### 5.3 Philanthropic Funding Shocks: Caritas and She's the First Exit in Child Protection Portfolio (₹52 Lakh Deficit)
In 2025, DEHAT experienced a significant institutional funding crisis within its human protection division:
- **Sudden Funding Retractions**: Two long-standing international development partners—**Caritas** and **She’s the First (STF)**—were unable to complete their committed multi-year grant tenures due to international funding shifts and internal organizational restructuring.
- **The ₹52 Lakh Programmatic Deficit**: These partner exits affected two major non-FASAL initiatives: the *Baal Prahri* child protection project (funded by Caritas; €90,195, 2023–25) and the *Panchi* adolescent girl empowerment project (funded by STF; ₹66,00,000, 2022–25), creating an immediate budgetary shortfall of approximately **₹52,00,000** in DEHAT's child and human protection operations.
- **Clear Institutional Delineation**: It is essential to delineate that this funding shock occurred within DEHAT’s parallel human protection and child rights programs. The **FASAL program, funded independently by Azim Premji Philanthropic Initiatives (APPI) under Grant G-2110-10970**, maintained its dedicated multi-year grant budget throughout this period.

### 5.4 Structural Downsizing and Staff Retrenchment: Impact on Collective Morale
The ₹52 Lakh funding deficit in DEHAT’s human protection division necessitated painful organizational contractions:
- **Geographic Scale-Down**: DEHAT was forced to scale down field interventions across **12 non-FASAL Gram Panchayats along the Indo-Nepal border** that had been supported under the Caritas and STF child protection grants.
- **Frontline Staff Retrenchment**: Most painfully, the organization had to execute **13 staff layoffs**, affecting experienced team members who had been associated with DEHAT for more than 15 years in its historical child protection and anti-trafficking work.
- **FASAL Operational Protection**: DEHAT safeguarded the core FASAL program from operational disruption:
  - The AAS network was consolidated from 80 to **74 active, high-performing collectives**. This consolidation was an intentional operational rationalization aimed at strengthening group functionality and participation (already targeted in monthly plans as early as January 2025 per Deck `12. NRM_DEHAT_जनवरी २०२५.pptx`, Slide 15), rather than an emergency response to budget cuts.
  - DEHAT secured bridge funding for its broader institutional programs from **World Neighbors (₹15 Lakh; DEHAT selected as the sole partner organization from Uttar Pradesh)** and an individual philanthropic family in Silicon Valley (**₹10 Lakh annually**).

### 6.1 Manju: Educational Leadership, Floodland Resilience, and Gender Vanguard
- **Geographic & Social Identity**: Resident of Mihinpurwa Block, Bahraich District (associated with Bhattha Bargadaha village in community records); smallholder farmer managing 3 Bighas of household land; active member of the Aajeevika Adhikar Sangathan (AAS).
- **Early Life of Deprivation and Abuse**:
  Manju’s early life was marked by acute loss. Following her father's death during her childhood, her mother worked tirelessly as a daily wage laborer to sustain the household and keep the children in school. Before Manju could complete her basic schooling, she was married off at a young age. Entering married life without awareness of her legal rights or personal agency, she endured years of emotional, physical, and social hardship. Her mobility was strictly restricted, decisions were made for her, and her daily existence was characterized by humiliation and fear. As she reflected: *"I thought this was my fate. I did not know that my life could be different."*
- **The Awakening: Peeping Through the Window to Grassroots Leadership**:
  A critical turning point occurred when women's meetings supported through the JAI AZADI / AAS program began in her village. Initially afraid to attend, Manju listened from her windows, observing facilitators who spoke about rights, equality, self-respect, and collective solidarity. Gathering courage, she stepped forward and joined the group. Finding a space where women shared experiences and supported one another, she came to understand that violence was not destiny and silence was not strength: *"When I heard other women speak, I realized I was not alone. That day something changed inside me."*
- **The Community Campaign for a Village School**:
  In her locality, children faced long, difficult walks along unsafe roads to reach the nearest primary school, leading many children to drop out. Determined that children should have access to education, Manju initiated an advocacy campaign that spanned nearly three years.
  Through interface meetings with officials at the village, block, and district levels, she made approximately 8 to 10 formal visits to administrative authorities. When progress stalled, she utilized public grievance systems, including the Chief Minister's *Jan Sunvayi* Helpline (1076), and raised the demand in village assemblies. Despite discouragement from skeptics who doubted that a rural woman could influence administrative decisions, Manju persisted: *"There were nights I cried, but the next morning I stood up again. I knew this fight was not only for my children, but for every child."* Her persistent advocacy bore fruit when administrative processes for village school construction were formally approved and commenced.
- **Agronomic Adaptation in the *Khala* Lowlands**:
  Manju’s household held 3 Bighas of land, of which 2 Bighas were situated in the *Khala* (a depressed, low-lying area) prone to severe waterlogging where standing crops frequently drowned. Drawing courage from her collective engagement, Manju and her family assessed the risk and decided to plant sugarcane across all three Bighas, intercropping seasonal vegetables using seeds and organic compost methods supported by DEHAT. When monsoon inundation arrived, the water-tolerant sugarcane survived the waterlogging. While noting that *"the income may be less, but the experience gained is very valuable"* (Grant Report 2025, p. 34), the low-lying plots ceased to be an economic liability and became a dependable source of household sustenance.
- **Current Vanguard Role**:
  Today, Manju is recognized as a dedicated grassroots leader. She travels outside her locality—visiting Bahraich, Lucknow, and Rajasthan (participating in the VAAGDHARA agro-ecology exposure visit in January 2026). She actively supports women experiencing domestic violence, speaks confidently in public meetings, and exemplifies how individual resilience and collective organization foster lasting community leadership.

### 6.2 Shanti Devi: Transforming Child Marriage Trauma into Collective Agency and Functional Literacy
- **Geographic & Social Identity**: Resident of Bajpur Bankati village, Mihinpurwa Block (documented in case records and Grant Report 2025); smallholder farmer, community facilitator, and mother.
- **Early Hardship, Physical Abuse, and Abandonment**:
  Shanti’s father passed away during her childhood, leaving her mother to support the household through grueling daily wage labor while striving to educate her children. Facing severe economic deprivation, Shanti was married off as a child. In her marital home, she was subjected to continuous physical abuse and emotional trauma over trivial household matters. Because her menstrual cycles were delayed and irregular, her marital family and husband subjected her to degrading verbal abuse. Eventually, her husband abandoned her entirely, leaving her destitute. She returned to her mother’s home, where she was treated by extended relatives and village society as an economic burden.
- **Maternal Tragedy and Compassionate Adoption**:
  After two years of separation, her husband returned, and Shanti subsequently gave birth to two children. Tragically, both young children passed away during early childhood. Shattered by profound grief, Shanti struggled to rebuild her emotional life.
  During this period of hardship, a woman in a neighboring village passed away during childbirth, leaving behind a newborn infant girl without parental care. In a profound act of maternal empathy, Shanti stepped forward and adopted the infant girl as her own daughter. Defying conservative village criticism, Shanti poured her life into caring for the child: *"This decision came from the heart, not out of any compulsion. With her arrival, my lonely life found its glow again. I became a mother once more"* (Grant Report 2025, p. 30).
- **Collective Solidarity and Literacy Transformation**:
  When DEHAT organized women's meetings in her locality, Shanti attended seeking community support, facing domestic violence at home for participating. At the meeting, she met fellow leader **Manju**, who offered personal support and encouragement: *"We will move forward like this; you are not alone."*
  Because Shanti could not read or write, she initially communicated using recorded audio voice notes. Manju urged her to acquire functional literacy: *"We cannot listen to your recordings in front of everyone. Learn to message."* Manju sat with Shanti, teaching her mobile messaging and functional literacy skills. Through persistent effort, Shanti learned to read and write.
- **Intergenerational Educational Breakthrough**:
  Determined that her adopted daughter would access educational opportunities, Shanti enrolled her in the residential **Kasturba Gandhi Balika Vidyalaya (KGBV)**, securing free residential education. Together with Manju, Shanti became among the first mothers from her village to enroll her daughter in KGBV, inspiring other families in the community to support their daughters' formal education.
- **Agro-Ecological Engagement & Civic Facilitation**:
  Shanti became actively involved in farming on her family land, participating in sustainable agricultural practices and homestead vegetable cultivation. Today, Shanti is a confident grassroots facilitator, supporting women facing domestic violence, encouraging participation in collective village platforms, and demonstrating how collective solidarity can rebuild lives shattered by trauma.

### 6.3 Rajkumari Devi: From Wage Labour to High-Yield Turmeric Leadership and Civic Recognition
- **Geographic & Social Identity**: Resident of Kailash Nagar village (Chahalwa GP, Sujauli Police Station, Mihinpurwa Block); husband: Shri Bhikhari; smallholder farmer and active AAS community leader.
- **Prior Agrarian Realities & Wage Labour**:
  Prior to the FASAL intervention, Rajkumari Devi and her family experienced persistent economic insecurity. Although possessing agricultural land, the household practiced conventional farming heavily reliant on expensive synthetic chemical fertilizers, commercial pesticides, and hybrid seeds. Escalating input costs combined with erratic rainfall frequently resulted in low crop margins, compelling Rajkumari Devi and her husband to work as manual agricultural wage laborers to sustain household expenses.
- **Capacity Building and Technical Mastery with DEHAT**:
  Rajkumari Devi’s farming trajectory transformed after joining the Aarti Aajivika Adhikar Sangathan (AAS) in Kailash Nagar. Through DEHAT's agronomic training workshops, she gained technical competencies in:
  - Scientific biological seed treatment using organic bio-inoculants prior to planting.
  - Basal soil enrichment using organic Farm Yard Manure (FYM) and Jeevamrit.
  - Raised-bed planting, geometric line spacing, and protective straw mulching.
  - Strategic crop diversification toward high-value commercial spices.
- **The Turmeric Breakthrough**:
  Allocating a plot measuring less than one Bigha to improved organic turmeric (*Curcuma longa*) intercropped with Colocasia (*Arbi*), Rajkumari Devi applied the complete LEISA package: organic raised beds, regular Jeevamrit drenches, and moisture-conserving straw mulching.
  Her plot achieved outstanding production, harvesting healthy rhizomes that yielded a net profit of approximately **₹60,000** from a sub-bigha plot (recorded in project documentation and Grant Report 2024). This demonstrated to smallholders across Kailash Nagar that organic spice cultivation could deliver strong cash income while avoiding synthetic input debts.
- **Commercial Crop Expansion**:
  Reinvesting agricultural returns, Rajkumari Devi diversified her household farm holdings into multi-year commercial crops:
  - **2 Bighas of commercial Banana cultivation**.
  - **1 Bigha of dedicated organic Turmeric cultivation**.
  These cash crops provided stable household income, reducing reliance on manual wage labor and supporting quality schooling for her children.
- **Civic Engagement and District Recognition**:
  Beyond farm production, Rajkumari Devi became an active civic participant in Kailash Nagar, speaking confidently in Gram Sabhas and encouraging other women farmers to adopt sustainable practices. In recognition of her agricultural accomplishments, she was awarded official **Certificates of Appreciation (*Prashasti Patra*) for Agricultural Excellence** presented by the local Member of Legislative Assembly (MLA) and the District Magistrate (DM) of Bahraich (Grant Report 2024, pp. 9 & 30).

### 6.4 Anarkali: Tribal Community Leadership, PDS Food Rights, and Agro-Ecological Farming
- **Geographic & Social Identity**: Resident of Rampurwa village, Fakirpuri Post, Mihinpurwa Block; member of the indigenous tribal community (*जनजाति*); Treasurer (*Koshadhyaksh*) of **Sadhana Azadi Aajeevika Adhikar Sangathan (AAS)**.
- **Overcoming Social Marginalization and Exclusion**:
  Coming from a marginalized tribal community along the forest periphery, Anarkali grew up in a social environment where tribal families faced structural neglect and discrimination from dominant local groups and public institutions. Tribal women were customarily expected to remain silent, and fear of engaging with government authorities was widespread. Despite these constraints, Anarkali sought avenues to organize community women and defend basic welfare rights.
- **Institutional Role as Treasurer of Sadhana Azadi AAS**:
  Recognizing her dedication, DEHAT organizers supported Anarkali in mobilizing women in Rampurwa, where she was chosen as Treasurer of the *Sadhana Azadi AAS*. Through rights awareness training, she recognized that individual complaints could easily be dismissed, but collective community organization provided the leverage needed to secure public entitlements.
- **Grassroots Entitlement Struggles in Rampurwa**:
  Anarkali participated in several significant rights struggles:
  1. *Public Distribution System (PDS) Food Rights Mobilization*: In Rampurwa and neighboring Fakirpuri hamlets, the local fair-price shop dealer routinely shorted poor families on their food rations, distributing only 3 kg of grain instead of the statutory 5 kg per unit. Anarkali, alongside fellow women leaders, organized community delegations to Tehsil Day, filing formal complaints before senior administrative officials. The resulting official inquiry uncovered widespread distribution irregularities, leading to the dismissal of the corrupt dealer and the restoration of full statutory grain rations to eligible families (FASAL Narrative Report HY2, pp. 8–9; Grant Report 2025, p. 35).
  2. *Advocating for Fair MGNREGA Implementation*: She actively monitored local public works, demanding that job cards be issued to eligible tribal women and wages be disbursed transparently and on schedule.
  3. *Securing PMAY Housing Sanctions*: Overcoming administrative bottlenecks, Anarkali assisted destitute families in filing housing applications, helping secure permanent pucca houses under Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana for multiple poor households.
- **Agro-Ecological Farming & Nutrition Gardens**:
  Anarkali established organic farming practices on her family plot, preparing home-made bio-inputs and developing a diverse homestead kitchen garden (*Poshan Vatika*). Her productive plots demonstrated the viability of chemical-free vegetable and grain production to neighboring farmers across Rampurwa.
- **District-Wide Community Recognition**:
  Today, Anarkali is recognized across Bahraich District as a respected community organizer. Her journey illustrates the transformative potential of grassroots collective organization, where indigenous women become active guardians of constitutional rights and agro-ecological resilience.

| Behavioral Dimension | Traditional Dependency Thinking (*Chali Aa Rahi Soch*) | Progressive Collective Consciousness (*Ab Ka Soch*) |

### 8.1 The Primary Survey Pipeline & Relational Taxonomy
The empirical foundation of the FASAL monitoring and evaluation system rests upon a multi-stage data collection, enumeration, and digitization pipeline designed to document the socio-ecological conditions of forest-fringe farming households. Data originated through the paper-based **Family Development Booklet (FDB)** (*परिवार विकास पुस्तिका*), a structured 16-page physical household tracking instrument administered in Hindi by community resource persons (Krishi Mitras) and field coordinators.

Crucially, methodological auditing of submission timestamps and survey architecture establishes that the household dataset does not represent three independent longitudinal panel waves. All 1,329 households were enumerated in a **single cross-sectional retrospective recall survey round** administered between 6 February 2025 and 3 November 2025 (with three pilot questionnaires initiated in July 2024). All submissions were uploaded through a centralized enumerator account (`shwetha05`), with a median form completion/transcription duration of approximately 296 minutes. Retrospective recall was utilized to collect multi-year data covering three agricultural production cycles (2022, 2023, and 2024) and six half-year recall blocks (January–June 2022 through July–December 2024).

### 8.3 Deconstructing the Raw KoboToolbox Proliferation (4,467 Columns)
A comparative architectural audit between the raw digital Kobo export (`Family_Development_Book_..._2026-06-22-14-05-16.xlsx`, 4,467 columns) and the consolidated analytical CSV (3,002 columns) reveals the computational mechanisms governing field data inflation and severe pipeline data loss:
1. **XLSForm Cascading Multipliers**: In the Kobo OpenRosa data model, dependent questions generate unique columnar branches for every conditional option in a cascade. In the geographical roster, the selection of `Village Panchayat` branched into **7 discrete `Village Name` columns** (one per GP cascade, which together cover 100% of the 1,329 households), and the selection of `Village` branched into **20 discrete `AAS Name` columns** (18 non-empty). Across these columns, exactly 18 distinct villages and 66 AAS groups are recorded.
2. **Multi-Select Array Expansion**: Dietary tracking questions stored both a concatenated string variable and individual binary indicator flags (e.g., `/greens`, `/ladyfinger`, `/banana`, `/Other`), expanding 36 monthly queries into 1,260 distinct columns (36 months × 35 columns per month = 3 text headers + 32 discrete food item flags).
3. **The Data Cleaning Pipeline Defect (SYS-1)**: During the programmatic generation of `DEHAT_Cleaned_Data.xlsx` (and the derived CSVs), the cleaning script encountered 404 repeated question labels across survey cycles. Rather than merging or uniquely renaming them, the deduplication logic retained **only the last physical occurrence** of each label and discarded **1,468 columns**. Crucially, the surviving column kept the positional header of the *first* occurrence:
   - For longitudinal modules (migration, livestock, scheme awareness), columns labeled "Jan–Jun 2022" actually contain Jul–Dec 2024 data, producing severe temporal dislocation.
   - For agricultural economics, **30 crop net-income columns** (10 per year: Rabi pea, coriander, garlic, onion; Zaid onion, cucumber, bitter gourd, cabbage, beetroot; groundnut) were completely dropped, erasing ₹22.28 Lakh in 2022, ₹36.33 Lakh in 2023, and ₹93.72 Lakh in 2024.
   - For administrative geography, only the last GP cascade column (Mangalpurwa, 88 households) was retained, leaving `Village Name` empty in 1,241 out of 1,329 rows (93.4% null) in the CSV, even though the raw Kobo data has 100% complete village coverage.
4. **Administrative Distribution Across Gram Panchayats**: While project narratives frequently refer to "6 Gram Panchayats", the household microdata records exactly **7 Gram Panchayats**:
   - **Karikot GP**: 602 Households (45.30%)
   - **Fakirpuri GP**: 229 Households (17.23%)
   - **Chahalwa GP**: 183 Households (13.77%)
   - **Bajpur Bankati GP**: 113 Households (8.50%)
   - **Mangalpurwa GP**: 88 Households (6.62%)
   - **Vishunapur GP**: 73 Households (5.49%)
   - **Badkhadiya GP**: 41 Households (3.09%)

## CONCLUSION: STRATEGIC SYNTHESIS AND LESSONS LEARNED

The empirical and qualitative synthesis of the FASAL program demonstrates that sustainable smallholder transformation in highly vulnerable, ecologically precarious forest-border environments cannot be achieved through techno-agronomic interventions alone.

FASAL’s lasting significance lies in its **integrated political-ecological architecture**:

1. **Democratic Unionization as the Precondition for Transformation**:
   By establishing an autonomous, three-tiered democratic federation (74–80 hamlet AAS collectives, 5 Cluster Federations, and the apex Sangharsh Block Level Federation) anchored in the doctrine of *"Jiska Mudda Uski Agwai"*, the program built the collective power necessary for marginalized Tharu, Dalit, and OBC smallholders to defend their rights. This organized agency challenged generational feudal coercion—including unpaid forced labour (*Begari*), informal harvest levies (*Tihai*), and public food distribution fraud.

2. **Ecological Agriculture Built on Mutual Aid**:
   With civic dignity established, participating families possessed the institutional confidence required to transition away from debt-inducing chemical inputs. Through Low External Input Sustainable Agriculture (LEISA), smallholders adopted on-farm biological soil care (*Jeevamrit*, *Ghanjeevamrit*), botanical non-pesticidal sprays (*Char Chatni*, *10 Parani Ark*, *Neemastra*), spatial intensification via vertical trellises (*Machan*), and high-value commercial spice value chains (organic turmeric intercropped with colocasia), significantly reducing out-of-pocket agricultural production costs.

3. **Measurable Upward Mobility Alongside Persistent Realities**:
   The external midline recall survey (analyzing 1,328 households) recorded meaningful economic progress: average annual crop income rose from ₹32,983 in 2022 to ₹54,993 in 2024 (+66.7%), with the proportion of households earning under ₹50,000 falling from 81.2% to 61.1%. However, an objective assessment acknowledges that **61.1% of farming households remained within the low-income bracket (<₹50,000/year)**, illustrating that deep-seated poverty across flood-prone forest buffers requires sustained, multi-year structural investment.

4. **Public Infrastructure and Entitlement Convergence**:
   Moving beyond isolated NGO project delivery, the federation mobilized **₹7.71 Crore (₹7,70,98,360)** in verified public entitlement and community infrastructure convergence across **577 genuine project items** from 2023 to 2025. This unlocked critical public investments—including 63 PMAY homes and 72 toilets in 2025, piped drinking water systems under the Jal Jeevan Mission, concrete flood-resilient access roads, and decentralized citizen services through the Jan Suvidha Kendra.

5. **Institutional Resilience and Feminist Democratic Governance**:
   Confronted with external funding transitions in DEHAT’s broader human protection division in 2025 (the exit of Caritas and She's the First), the organization protected the core FASAL APPI program, consolidated AAS groups to 74 active collectives, and accelerated a multi-year governance transition toward decentralized feminist collective leadership. Initiated in May 2021 following the tragic passing of founder Dr. Jitendra Chaturvedi, this governance model established shared executive stewardship, prioritized frontline staff mental health, and strengthened institutional oversight.

The FASAL experience provides a grounded, evidence-based model for community-led agro-ecological resilience, showing how marginalized forest-fringe communities can build democratic institutions, reclaim constitutional entitlements, and lead sustainable agrarian change.
